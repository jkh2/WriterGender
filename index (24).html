How it works, and what it can't do

Every category below is counted as a share of the words in the sample, compared against how often those words appear in English generally, and then weighted by how strongly that category separated men from women in the published research. The weights come from Newman, Groom, Handelman and Pennebaker (2008), who ran 14,324 text samples through standardized language categories and reported an effect size for each one. The largest gaps were in function words — pronouns, articles, prepositions — not in what people wrote about.

  • Every effect here is small. In the source study only five categories reached even the conventional threshold for a "small" effect. These are population averages; the authors write that predictions about individuals "should be made cautiously, if at all."
  • The accuracy of this particular tool has not been measured. Comparable published tools run 60–70% accurate. This one uses its own word lists, so that figure is not inherited. The Test tab exists to establish a real number.
  • It measures a lean, not a person. A result is a statement about the writing. Field, education, nationality, age and audience all move these numbers. Professional and academic writing tends to read male whoever wrote it.
  • For AI text it measures the persona, not the model. A model's style comes from its training and its prompt. If the lean changes when the prompt changes, the prompt is what you measured.
  • Poor fits: poetry, lyrics, lists, quoted or heavily edited text, and anything under 300 words. Remove quoted passages before analyzing.

The word lists are our own compilation, published in the page source. Method after Argamon, Koppel, Fine and Shimoni (2003), who first showed that word usage could predict author sex; no code or word list from any existing tool is used here. Nothing you paste leaves your browser.
